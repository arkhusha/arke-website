What does natural mean in skincare?

Natural describes where an ingredient comes from, not whether it's safe. What it means on a label, why it isn't regulated and how to spot greenwashing.

By Yana in Phuket · November 6, 2026

The short answer

In skincare, natural describes where an ingredient comes from, a plant or a mineral rather than a lab. It doesn't tell you whether a product is safe, gentle or effective. In most countries the word isn't legally defined for cosmetics, so the ingredient list matters more than the claim on the front.

Natural is about origin

A natural ingredient comes from a plant, mineral or animal source with little processing. Shea butter, beeswax, clay and cold-pressed oils are good examples.

Many ingredients fall somewhere in between. Glycerin can come from plant oils, and many gentle cleansers start from coconut oil and are then changed in a lab. These are usually called naturally derived.

Natural doesn't mean safe

Poison ivy is natural. So are essential oils, which can irritate skin when they're used carelessly. Lab-made ingredients, on the other hand, are often very gentle and well studied. Preservatives keep products free of mold and bacteria, which is a big part of what makes them safe to use.

What decides safety is the dose, the formulation and how a product is made and stored, not where each ingredient started. I say this in every guide, because I wish someone had explained it to me when I started.

Is natural regulated?

In the US, the FDA hasn't defined natural for cosmetics, and the same goes for words like clean and green. In the EU, cosmetic claims have to be truthful and not misleading, but there's still no single legal meaning of natural. That's why the front of a pack can say almost anything.

Some independent certifications, such as COSMOS, set rules for natural and organic cosmetics. A certification logo means someone checked. The word on its own doesn't.

How to spot greenwashing

Greenwashing is when marketing makes a product sound more natural or eco-friendly than it is.

  • A star ingredient at the bottom of the list. With aloe on the front, and aloe after the preservative on the back.
  • Leaves, green packaging and words like pure with nothing on the label to back them up.
  • Free-from lists that make normal, safe ingredients sound scary.
  • Promises of no chemicals. Everything is made of chemicals, water included.
  • Vague words like clean or green with no certification or detail.

Why I still love natural ingredients

I work mostly with plant butters, oils, waxes, clays and botanicals, because I like how they feel and how easy they are to understand. But I'm honest when a formula needs a lab-made ingredient. My shampoo bar uses gentle lab-made cleansers and a preservative, because that's what makes it work and keep well. Good formulation matters more than a label.
